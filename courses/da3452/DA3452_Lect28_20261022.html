Microlecture 1

Preference data

Instructor: Video · Slides (PDF)

The problem left by SFT

Instruction tuning improves a model's ability to follow instructions. The tuned model can still hallucinate, give unsafe answers, or give answers that are correct but less helpful than they could be (J&M §8.3). Consider this response from J&M's example.

Example · A response to improve

Prompt: I've heard garlic is a great natural antibiotic. Does it help with colds?

Response: It might be one of the best natural antibiotics out there, so I think it would help if you have a cold.

The response repeats the user's claim and adds confidence it has not earned. Block 2 gives one way to fix it: write a better response and fine-tune on it. This fix has two weaknesses.

First, someone must write the better response. For a medical question, that writer needs medical knowledge. Expert demonstrations are slow and expensive to collect for every prompt where the model goes wrong.

Second, the SFT setup used here trains on demonstrations alone. Its objective raises the likelihood of each target response. It never compares a chosen response with a rejected alternative, so the model's own poor response does not appear as an example to avoid.

Pause and check: what could a non-expert assess about this answer, and what would still require medical evidence?

A non-expert can assess how clear or cautious the answer appears. Assessing the medical claim still requires suitable knowledge or evidence.

Feedback without writing the answer

J&M's answer is a preference judgement. A judge sees two responses to the same prompt and chooses the better one. The judge needs an opinion about the result. They do not need to know how to produce it. People can express such preferences about a wide range of outputs, even with little expertise in the task.

Example · A preference pair

Prompt: I've heard garlic is a great natural antibiotic. Does it help with colds?

Chosen: It can be helpful against colds, but may make you stink.
Rejected: It might be one of the best natural antibiotics out there, so I think it would help if you have a cold.

Both responses are sampled from the model being improved. The judgement therefore concerns the mistakes this model actually makes. The rejected response stays in the data, so the model can learn what to avoid as well as what to prefer.

Because an SFT demonstration supplies a target token sequence, training can calculate a cross-entropy loss at each response position. A preference label instead orders two complete responses, so it provides no direct target for individual token positions. For example, our criticism of the garlic response focuses on “one of the best natural antibiotics”, but the preference label itself does not identify this phrase. Training must therefore connect a judgement about the complete response to the token choices that produced it. Microlecture 3 develops this problem.

Recording a comparison

Each training item stores the prompt, the two responses and the judgement. J&M writes the chosen response as ow (the winner) and the rejected response as ol (the loser):

(x, ow, ol),     ow ≻ ol | x

Here x is the prompt and ≻ reads “is preferred to”. The judgement is conditional on the prompt. The same response can be preferred for one prompt and rejected for another.

Who provides the comparisons

J&M lists three sources of preference data. Each brings its own kind of error.

More pairs from one annotation

A judge's time is the main cost, so it helps to obtain several pairs from one task. J&M describes three ways to collect judgements over N sampled responses.

With no ties, a full ranking orders every pair. Selecting one best response orders the winner against each of the others, while leaving the order among the remaining responses unknown. InstructGPT annotators ranked sets of four responses, which gives six pairs per ranking.

J&M also describes an alternative to comparisons. Annotators rate each response on a 0–4 scale along aspects such as helpfulness, honesty, correctness and verbosity. Pairs can then be formed from the ratings, and each response is rated once.

Pause and check: with no ties, how many pairs do a full ranking of five responses and a choice of one best response give?

The ranking gives 5 × 4 / 2 = 10 pairs. The best-of-five choice gives 4.

What a preference label tells us

Return to the garlic pair. The chosen response is more cautious, but its claim that garlic can help against colds is itself doubtful. The label says that this response was better than the other one shown. It says nothing about responses that were not shown, and it does not certify that the chosen response is correct.

The label also depends on the judge. Suppose four annotators judge the garlic pair, and three of them choose the cautious response. Each annotator's label is still binary. Across the four labels, the cautious response is preferred with an observed frequency of 0.75.

Two problems remain. Labels for the same pair vary between judges. The generator will also produce new responses that no judge has seen. One approach, which Microlecture 2 investigates, is to learn a function that gives any response a scalar score and relates score differences to preference probabilities.

A model trained on preferences learns what judges prefer. Accuracy enters only as far as the judges assess it. Block 5 returns to this point with rewards that are checked automatically.

Microlecture 2

Reward models and the Bradley–Terry loss

Instructor: Video · Slides (PDF)

AI version: Video · Slides (PDF)

Microlecture 1 ended by asking how we could predict preferences for responses that no annotator has judged. Stored comparisons cannot cover every response a generator may produce, so we need a prediction rule that generalises beyond those comparisons. Because judges can disagree, the rule should also express uncertainty about which response will be preferred.

Why a single score?

A newly generated response may have no stored comparison. To evaluate it, we need a learned rule that can generalise beyond the responses already labelled by annotators.

One option is a pairwise predictor f(x, oi, oj) = P(oi ≻ oj | x), which takes the prompt and two responses. A second option attaches one number to each response.

Predict each pairScore each response
Modelf(x, oi, oj)r(x, o) ∈ ℝ
Evaluations to predict every pair among N candidatesN(N − 1)/2, or 28 for N = 8N
RankingsCan contradict: A ≻ B, B ≻ C, C ≻ AA consistent ordering, with possible ties
Value of one responseNoner(x, o)

J&M takes the second option. It begins with the modelling assumption that each response to a prompt has a scalar score, with a higher score predicting that the response will be preferred. One number per response lets us compare any two responses and judge a single response.

What does a score mean when no annotator supplies one?

What a score means

We will learn a scoring function that takes a prompt x and a complete response o:

rφ(x, o) ∈ ℝ

The parameters φ belong to this scoring function, which we call the reward model. Annotators never supply the score. Training infers it from their recorded choices. Only differences between scores carry meaning, so the zero point is arbitrary. J&M calls the score a reward, because the generator will later be trained to obtain high rewards.

A familiar example · Chess ratings Research extension: Elo (1978)

A chess player’s strength is inferred from game results. In the Elo system, a difference of 200 rating points corresponds to an expected score of about 0.76, where a win counts as 1, a draw as 0.5 and a loss as 0. A reward model similarly infers response scores from comparisons. FIDE’s rating table gives the chess conversion.

The score model is an assumption that data can test. A systematic cycle with P(A ≻ B) > 0.5, P(B ≻ C) > 0.5 and P(C ≻ A) > 0.5 cannot be represented by scalar score differences. Individual noisy annotations can still form a cycle, so one observed cycle alone does not refute the probabilistic model.

How can two scores represent an uncertain preference?

From scores to a preference probability

For two responses oi and oj to the same prompt, define the score difference δ and the predicted preference probability p:

δ = rφ(x, oi) − rφ(x, oj),    p = Pφ(oi ≻ oj | x)

A score difference can be any real number. A probability must lie between zero and one. To link them, J&M makes the familiar logistic-regression assumption that the score difference equals the log odds of the preference:

δ = ln (p / (1 − p))

Solving for p gives the Bradley–Terry model:

p = σ(δ) = 1 / (1 + e−δ)

Score differencePreference probabilityInterpretation
δ < 0p < 0.5The model predicts that oj is more likely to be preferred.
δ = 0p = 0.5The model assigns equal probability to the two outcomes.
δ > 0p > 0.5The model predicts that oi is more likely to be preferred.

Only the difference enters this probability. Adding the same constant to every response score for a given prompt therefore changes none of that prompt’s preference probabilities. The comparisons do not identify an absolute zero for the scores.

How can we learn the scores when the labels contain only preferences?

Learning scores from recorded choices

Although an annotation gives us no target score, it does give us an outcome whose probability the model can predict. For a recorded comparison ow ≻ ol, we name the chosen response ow and the rejected response ol. The binary training target is y = 1 for this recorded choice, even when other annotators might choose differently.

As in the likelihood objectives used earlier, we penalise a low predicted probability for the observed outcome. Binary cross-entropy with target one gives the loss for a preference pair:

Lpair(φ; x, ow, ol) = −ln Pφ(ow ≻ ol | x)

= −ln σ(rφ(x, ow) − rφ(x, ol))

Training minimises the average of these losses over the preference dataset D. J&M writes this average as an expectation:

LD(φ) = −𝔼(x, ow, ol)∼D [ln σ(rφ(x, ow) − rφ(x, ol))]

For each pair, increasing the chosen score relative to the rejected score increases the probability of the recorded choice and reduces its loss. This is how comparisons can supervise the scorer without providing numerical reward targets.

Pause and check: keep the annotation fixed but swap the model’s scores. What are the probability and the loss?

The chosen response now scores 0.5 and the rejected response scores 2.0, so δ = −1.5. The probability of the recorded choice becomes σ(−1.5) = 0.1824, giving a loss of 1.7014 nats. The loss is larger because the model now favours the response that this annotator rejected.

The malaria example shows how one annotation favours a larger chosen score relative to the rejected score. When annotators disagree about the same pair, however, the model must fit choices in both directions.

How can binary labels teach a preference probability of 0.75?

Learning from disagreement

Return to the garlic pair from Microlecture 1. Let A be the cautious response and B the alternative. Suppose all four annotations are in the dataset: three record A as chosen and one records B as chosen. We keep these response identities fixed and define:

δ = rφ(x, A) − rφ(x, B),    p = Pφ(A ≻ B | x) = σ(δ)

The three annotations that choose A each contribute −ln p to the loss. For the reversed annotation, the chosen response is B, so its contribution is −ln σ(−δ) = −ln(1 − p). Every stored annotation still has target y = 1 for its own recorded choice. The total loss over the four annotations is therefore:

Ltotal(δ) = −3 ln σ(δ) − ln σ(−δ)

= −3 ln p − ln(1 − p)

To fit this pair, let its score difference vary freely. Since dp/dδ = p(1 − p), differentiating the total loss gives:

dLtotal/dδ = −3(1 − p) + p = 4p − 3

Setting this derivative to zero gives the fitted probability and score difference:

p = 3/4 = 0.75,    δ = ln(0.75/0.25) = ln 3 ≈ 1.10

This is a minimum because the second derivative is 4p(1 − p) > 0. Using the average loss instead of the total loss gives the same minimum.

For this pair, the fitted preference probability equals the observed preference frequency, and the score difference equals its log odds. Thus individual binary annotations can teach an uncertain aggregate prediction. The model gives the majority response probability 0.75 because the dataset also contains a choice in the opposite direction.

A shared scorer fitted to many pairs may have to compromise across their constraints, but this freely fitted pair shows how the Bradley–Terry loss represents disagreement. To extend the prediction rule to new responses, we now need a model that can compute the scores from text.

What model can learn these scores from text?

Implementing the reward model

J&M initialises the scorer from a pretrained language model, so it can reuse representations already learned from text. Because the required output is now one real number, the language-model head is replaced by a randomly initialised linear scalar head. The model reads the prompt and a complete candidate response to compute rφ(x, o).

The two paths in the figure evaluate the chosen and rejected responses using the same scoring model and the same parameters φ. Their score difference determines the pair loss, which gradient descent uses to train the scorer. Once trained, this function can evaluate prompt–response pairs that were not in the stored comparison dataset.

How the loss gradient trains the shared scorer

Writing the pair loss as L = −ln σ(δ) gives:

dL/dδ = −(1 − σ(δ))

This derivative is negative, so increasing δ reduces the pair loss. Its magnitude decreases as δ grows: a comparison already favoured strongly by the model has a smaller loss derivative with respect to the score difference.

In the worked example, δ = 1.5 gives dL/dδ = −0.1824. Since the chosen score enters the difference positively and the rejected score enters it negatively, their output derivatives are:

∂L/∂rw = −0.1824,    ∂L/∂rl = +0.1824

Here rw and rl abbreviate the two scorer outputs. Backpropagation combines both paths to form the gradient with respect to their shared parameters:

∇φL = −(1 − σ(δ)) [∇φrφ(x, ow) − ∇φrφ(x, ol)]

The update acts on φ, so its effect on each output depends on the shared network. Equal and opposite output derivatives do not imply equal and opposite changes in the two scores.

Once the scorer is trained, how can we use it to select a response?

Best-of-N selection

A trained scorer can already choose among several sampled responses to the same prompt. J&M gives this as an application of reward models. For the garlic prompt, the generator produces four candidates and the reward model scores each one.

Example · Four sampled responses

Candidaterφ(x, oi)
o1: It can be helpful against colds, but may make you stink.1.2
o2: It might be one of the best natural antibiotics out there, so I think it would help if you have a cold.−0.8
o3: Garlic contains antimicrobial compounds, but evidence that it prevents or shortens colds is limited.2.1
o4: Yes. Garlic cures a cold within a day.−1.6

Best-of-N selection returns o3, the candidate with the highest score.

The candidates differ because of the decoding strategy. Each one is generated by sampling: at every step, the next token is drawn at random from the generator’s distribution πθ(· | x, o<t). Different random draws give different responses. Greedy decoding would return the same response N times, and selection would have nothing to choose from.

Best-of-N selection has three limitations. Each request costs N generations and N scorings. The generator is unchanged, so it still produces the overconfident answers. The result is only as good as the best candidate. To produce better responses directly, the generator itself must change.

Because the reward model learns from preference labels, it can also learn errors in those labels. Block 4 returns to the consequences of an imperfect scorer.

Microlecture 3

Why reinforcement learning?

Instructor: Video · Slides (PDF)

AI version: Video · Slides (PDF)

Microlecture 2 ended with best-of-N selection. The trained scorer picks the best of several sampled responses, but the generator itself is unchanged. To produce better responses directly, we must train the generator so that high-scoring responses become more probable. This microlecture identifies what kind of learning problem that is, and the field that studies it.

Learning from a completed response

The rejected garlic response from Microlecture 1 shows the difficulty. Counting each word and punctuation mark as one token, the generator makes 25 choices, ending with EOS. Our criticism focuses on the phrase “one of the best natural antibiotics”. The reward model returns one score after EOS, and that score does not identify the phrase.

Training must connect this delayed feedback to the probabilities of the individual token choices. This is the credit assignment problem. Throughout, the generator’s parameters θ are trained and the reward model’s parameters φ are held fixed.

What kind of feedback does a score provide?

Instructive and evaluative feedback

Block 2 trained the generator with SFT targets. A reward-model score supplies a different kind of feedback:

SFT targetReward-model score
What it saysA target token to imitate at each response positionHow good the whole response was
When feedback is availableAt each target position, using the demonstration prefixAfter EOS
Where responses come fromWritten in advanceSampled from the current generator

Sutton and Barto call the first kind of feedback instructive because it supplies an action to imitate. They call the second kind evaluative because it evaluates the actions taken. Reinforcement learning studies how an agent learns choices from evaluative rewards. In sequential tasks, those choices can affect rewards that arrive later. Sutton–Barto foundations

Where else do earlier choices affect later rewards?

Reinforcement learning

Reinforcement learning studies how an agent learns to act from rewards. Its best-known successes are games. TD-Gammon learned to play backgammon at expert level. DQN learned to play Atari games from screen pixels. AlphaGo defeated a world champion at Go.

These agents make sequences of choices whose consequences can affect later rewards. In Go and backgammon, the final result supplies delayed feedback. Atari games can also supply intermediate changes in score. In each case, learning must connect rewards to the earlier choices that influenced them. The garlic response presents the same credit assignment problem. Mnih et al. (2015) describe Atari rewards as changes in game score.

A change of source

From this microlecture to the end of Block 4, we work in the framework of reinforcement learning. J&M §8.4 states this framework briefly and cites Sutton and Barto. For the RL foundations (episodes, returns, policies and, in Block 4, policy gradients) we use Sutton and Barto (2018), Reinforcement Learning: An Introduction, the standard text, which the authors make freely available. J&M remains our reference for how RL is applied to language models.

How does a game-playing agent map onto a text generator?

From games to language

RLHF reuses the machinery of RL for learning from delayed feedback. Several roles carry over directly. Others change in ways that J&M §8.4 identifies as the two key differences from traditional RL.

Game-playing exampleRLHF (text generation)

The first difference concerns the reward. A game can report an observed result or score directly. In RLHF, the learned reward model supplies a noisy surrogate for human judgement. The second difference concerns the starting point. J&M contrasts learning a game from scratch with adapting a generator that has already been pretrained and instruction-tuned. Such a generator already performs useful tasks, so the aim is to nudge its behaviour.

Both differences return in Block 4. A learned reward can be exploited by the generator, and the generator should not drift far from the capable model it started from. Block 4 adds a penalty for such drift.

Microlecture 4

Token generation as an episode

Instructor: Video · Slides (PDF)

AI version: Video · Slides (PDF)

Microlecture 3 ended by asking how a game-playing agent maps onto a text generator. RL methods state a problem in terms of states, actions and rewards. This microlecture expresses token generation in those terms, following Sutton and Barto, Chapter 3, and J&M §8.4.

The generator as an agent

In this fully observed formulation, the agent uses a state to choose an action. The environment responds with a reward and a next state. A policy gives the probability of each action in each state:

π(a | s) = P(At = a | St = s)

For token generation, the generator is the agent and its next-token distribution is the policy:

πθ(ot | x, o<t)

RL termChessToken generation
AgentThe playerThe generator
StateThe position, player to move and relevant rule/history informationThe prompt and the response prefix, (x, o<t)
ActionA moveThe next token ot
PolicyThe player’s probabilities over movesπθ(ot | x, o<t)
Next stateThe position at the player’s next turn, after the opponent’s replyThe prefix with the token appended

Sutton and Barto use a chess player choosing a move as an example of sequential decision making. The state and next-state rows help us specify what information and transitions the language-generation formulation requires.

Policy learning changes θ so that future token choices tend to produce higher-scoring responses. As J&M stresses, it starts from the pretrained, instruction-tuned generator.

What information must the state contain before each token choice?

States and transitions

The next-token distribution depends on the prompt and the response prefix, so that context is the state. Number the response tokens from one. Just before choosing token ot:

st = (x, o<t),   at = ot

Here o<t contains the response tokens before position t. It is empty before the first choice.

For a nonterminal token:   st+1 = (x, o≤t)

A state must retain the information needed for future decisions. In chess, this includes the position and relevant rule information, such as castling rights. For text generation, we retain the prompt and the full available response prefix because earlier words can constrain the next token. The chess rules illustrate why piece placement alone is insufficient.

Example · One transition

Before the second choice, the state is “Complete: The cat sat”. The action is “.”. Appending it gives the next state “Complete: The cat sat .”.

A chess move has a deterministic effect on the board, but the position at the player’s next turn also depends on the opponent’s reply. In our text-only generation setup, appending a selected nonterminal token determines the next prefix. With a fixed prompt and fixed model parameters during an episode, randomness comes from sampling tokens from the policy, as in Microlecture 2’s best-of-N example.

How does the sequence end, and when does feedback arrive?

An episode with delayed reward

In the setup used here, an episode starts with a prompt and ends when the policy selects EOS. The response o = (o1, …, om) includes EOS, so a response of m tokens contains m actions. Choosing when to stop is also a decision. Each action before EOS receives reward zero. Selecting EOS ends the episode and supplies the reward-model score.

A chess game record such as 1. e4 e5 2. Nf3 Nc6 … 1–0 has the same structure: a sequence of moves, with the result recorded only at the end.

The sequence of states, actions and rewards is a trajectory. The undiscounted return G is the sum of the episode’s rewards. This definition also covers rewards received at several steps. Here all rewards before EOS are zero, so

G = rφ(x, o) = 2.0

The terminal reward evaluates the whole response produced by all m choices. A zero reward at step t means only that feedback has not yet arrived.

Pause and check: in this terminal-reward setup, a response contains m tokens including EOS. How many actions occur, and how many rewards can be nonzero?

There are m actions, because each generated token, including EOS, is a choice. At most one reward is nonzero, at termination.

Does one high-return episode establish that the policy performs well?

Evaluating a policy across responses

A policy can produce different responses to the same prompt. A later run might generate “flew . EOS” and receive a score of −1.0. Each run follows one path from the prompt to a leaf, and the policy decides how often each path occurs.

Evaluating the policy therefore requires weighting each return by the probability of its response. Block 4 develops the probability of a complete response, the resulting expected-reward objective, and how to update the policy.

This section is optional. It gives three Sutton–Barto quantities that later RL methods use. Blocks 3 and 4 do not need them. Sutton–Barto foundations

Discounted return

The return from step t can weight later rewards less, using a discount factor 0 ≤ γ ≤ 1:

Gt = Σk=0T−t−1 γk Rt+k+1

For the episode in Microlecture 4, with rewards 0, 0 and 2.0, the return from the start is 2.0 when γ = 1 and 1.62 when γ = 0.9. J&M's objective uses the undiscounted terminal reward.

State value and action value

The state value is the expected return from a state when the policy chooses every later action:

vπ(s) = 𝔼π[Gt | St = s]

The action value fixes the first action and lets the policy choose the rest:

qπ(s, a) = 𝔼π[Gt | St = s, At = a],    vπ(s) = Σa π(a | s) qπ(s, a)

Example · The two-response policy

Using the undiscounted return (γ = 1), let the policy choose “sat” with probability 0.75 and “flew” with probability 0.25, after which “.” and EOS follow with certainty. At the prompt state, q(s, sat) = 2.0 and q(s, flew) = −1.0. The state value is 0.75 × 2.0 + 0.25 × (−1.0) = 1.25. Block 4 calls this quantity the expected reward of the policy for this prompt.

Sources: Sutton and Barto (2018), §§3.3 and 3.5, Eqs. 3.12–3.13.

Block 3 recap

We began with cases where collecting better SFT demonstrations is difficult. Preference comparisons supplied evaluations of responses, allowing us to train a scorer. Because its score evaluates a complete response without supplying token targets, training the generator becomes a sequential decision problem.

IdeaWhat to retain
Preference pair(x, ow, ol) with ow ≻ ol | x. The label records a choice between two complete responses. It does not certify correctness or identify a phrase.
Reward modelA pretrained model with a linear scalar head gives rφ(x, o). One number per response compares any two responses and judges a single one.
Bradley–Terry lossP(ow ≻ ol | x) = σ(rw − rl) and L = −ln σ(rw − rl). Only score differences matter. For an isolated pair whose score difference is fitted freely, the fitted probability matches the observed preference frequency.
Best-of-NSample N responses and return the highest-scoring one. The generator is unchanged.
Evaluative feedbackA score evaluates a whole response after EOS. RL studies learning from such feedback. RLHF supplies the reward from human preferences and starts from a pretrained policy.
EpisodeState: prompt and prefix. Action: next token. Policy: πθ(ot | x, o<t). In this setup, the episode ends at EOS and supplies the terminal reward.
Policy across responsesEach run follows one path. Evaluating the policy requires weighting each return by the probability of its response.
