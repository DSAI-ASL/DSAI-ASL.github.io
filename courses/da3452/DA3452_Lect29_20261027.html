Microlecture 1

Response probability and expected reward

Instructor: Video · Slides (PDF)

Evaluating the generator

Module 5 described token generation as an episode. The state contains the prompt and the response prefix. The generator chooses the next token, and selecting EOS ends the episode. In our undiscounted setup, rewards before EOS are zero, so the episode’s return equals the score of its completed response.

Continue with the prompt Complete: The cat. Here sat, flew, . and EOS are individual tokens.

The responses carried forward from Module 5
Completed responseReturn
sat . EOS2.0
flew . EOS−1.0

These returns tell us how the scorer evaluates each response. Evaluating the generator also requires the probability of each response. A policy that usually produces the higher-scoring response has a different average return from one that usually produces the lower-scoring response.

We therefore need a probability for each complete response. The language model already supplies probabilities for individual token choices. We must connect those choices to the probability of the whole sequence.

How often does the policy generate each complete response?

Probability of a complete response

To generate sat . EOS, the policy must first choose sat, then choose . in the context containing sat, and finally choose EOS in the context containing sat .. The probability of this exact completed response is the product of those conditional probabilities.

πθ(sat . EOS | x) = πθ(sat | x)
× πθ(. | x, sat)
× πθ(EOS | x, sat .)

This is the same probability chain rule used earlier for language modelling. Each factor conditions on the prompt and all preceding response tokens.

Write a completed response as o = (o1, …, om), where the final token om is EOS. Then

πθ(o | x) = m∏t = 1πθ(ot | x, o<t)

Here x is the prompt, o<t is the response prefix before choice t, and θ denotes the generator’s parameters. The product on the right combines the contextual token probabilities into the whole-response probability on the left.

Why EOS belongs in the product

The probability of producing the prefix sat . includes continuations that keep generating after the full stop. The probability of the exact completed response sat . EOS must also include the choice to stop there. After a full stop, generation can continue until the policy selects EOS.

What probabilities does our example policy assign to its completed responses?

The two-response policy

Now specify the policy completely. At the first choice, it selects sat with probability 0.75 or flew with probability 0.25. After either first token, . follows with probability 1, and EOS then follows with probability 1. No other responses are possible under this policy.

Complete-response probabilities
ResponseProduct of conditional probabilitiesResponse probability
sat . EOS0.75 × 1 × 10.75
flew . EOS0.25 × 1 × 10.25

For a policy that reaches EOS with probability 1, the completed-response probabilities sum to 1:

∑oπθ(o | x) = 1

The policy’s token distributions determine these response probabilities. The expected-reward calculation will use them to weight the response scores.

How does this 0.75 differ from the 0.75 in Module 5?

Module 5 used three preferences out of four to fit a probability that a judge prefers one response to another. Here 0.75 is the probability that the generator produces sat . EOS. The probabilities refer to different events: a preference judgement and a sampled response.

What return should we expect when the policy generates responses repeatedly?

Expected reward for one prompt

A response that occurs more often contributes more to the policy’s average return. We therefore multiply each response’s return by its probability and add the contributions.

Call this quantity Jx(θ): the expected reward of the policy for the fixed prompt x. For a general policy, the same calculation sums over every possible completed response:

Jx(θ) =∑oπθ(o | x) rϕ(x, o)

The subscript x records that we have fixed the prompt. The response probability depends on θ, so changing the generator can change the weights in this sum. The reward-model parameters ϕ stay fixed during the policy updates.

Expectation and a single episode

The expectation is 1.25. An episode follows one response path and returns either 2.0 or −1.0. The expectation weights the possible episode returns by their response probabilities.

If we sample many episodes with the policy held fixed, their average return estimates this expectation. A finite sample can differ from 1.25 because response counts vary across samples.

Check a finite sample

Suppose two sampled episodes produce sat . EOS and flew . EOS. Their sample average is

Jsample = (2 + (−1)) / 2= 0.5

The policy’s expected reward is still 1.25. The observed average of these two draws is 0.5 because the proportions in a finite sample can differ from the policy’s probabilities.

How do we evaluate the policy across different prompts?

Expected reward across prompts

The quantity Jx(θ) evaluates one prompt. Training should improve responses across the prompts that matter for the task. Let Dprompt specify the distribution from which those prompts are drawn.

We first draw a prompt x from Dprompt, then sample a completed response o from the policy conditioned on that prompt, and finally obtain its reward-model score. Averaging over both choices gives

J(θ) =𝔼x ∼ Dprompt [Jx(θ)]
= 𝔼x ∼ Dprompt, o ∼ πθ(· | x) [rϕ(x, o)]

The first expression averages each prompt’s expected reward. The second writes the same procedure directly as an expectation over prompts and sampled responses. Prompt weights follow Dprompt; they are equal only when that distribution is uniform.

This is the expected-reward objective in J&M §8.4, Eq. 8.5. Our notation makes the prompt distribution explicit and writes the fixed scorer as rϕ. Policy learning aims to choose θ that maximises J(θ).

The objective increases when the policy places more probability on higher-scoring responses, after accounting for the prompt weights. This change comes from the probability weights in the expectation.

How can a policy parameter change the expected reward?

Parameterising the example policy

To improve the policy, we need to calculate how changes in its parameters affect expected reward. We first express the two-response policy using one scalar parameter. This gives a differentiable objective whose derivative we will calculate in ML02.

Return to our two-response policy. Only its first choice is uncertain. Let p be the probability of choosing sat; the probability of flew is then 1 − p. We continue to hold the later . and EOS choices fixed at probability 1.

A softmax over these two first-choice tokens depends on the difference between their logits. If their logits are ℓsat and ℓflew, define the difference as

z = ℓsat − ℓflew

The probability of sat is therefore

p = πθ(sat | x) = σ(z)
σ(z) = 11 + e−z

Here z is a scalar parameter for this policy. The full language model has many parameters θ, which determine its logits at every context. This scalar example lets us examine how a parameter affects the objective.

Substitute p and 1 − p into the expected-reward calculation:

Jx(z) = 2p + (−1)(1 − p)
= 3p − 1 = 3σ(z) − 1

The term −1 is the return when flew is chosen. Increasing p transfers probability to sat, whose return is higher by 3. This explains why the objective is 3p − 1.

Microlecture 2

The policy gradient

Instructor: Video · Slides (PDF)

AI version: Video · Slides (PDF)

The source of the generator gradient

ML01 ended with a parameterised policy and its expected reward. We now need a direction in parameter space that increases this objective. The prompt distribution and the reward-model parameters ϕ stay fixed.

Generation passes through a discrete sampling step. Backpropagation can differentiate the scorer with respect to continuous embeddings, but ordinary backpropagation cannot differentiate through sampled discrete token choices. A small change in θ either leaves a sampled token unchanged or switches it to another token, so a sampled token ID has no derivative with respect to θ.

For a fixed prompt and a fixed completed response, the frozen score does not depend on the generator parameters:

∇θ rϕ(x, o) = 0

The expected reward does depend on θ, and it depends on it smoothly, through the response probabilities. Increasing the probability of sat . EOS moves probability away from flew . EOS. The reward difference is 3, so this transfer raises the expectation. The expected reward is therefore the quantity we differentiate.

How fast does the expected reward change with z in our example?

Derivatives in the example

Recall the logit difference z from ML01. The probability of sat is p = σ(z), and the objective is Jx(z) = 3σ(z) − 1. We compute every derivative of this example once. Later sections reuse these values.

dpdz = p(1 − p),d ln pdz = 1 − p
dJxdz = 3p(1 − p)

At the current policy, z = ln 3 and p = 0.75, so dJx/dz = 0.5625. The slope is positive, so increasing z raises expected reward. The factor 3 is the reward gap between sat and flew.

The derivative for flew . EOS is the negative of the one for sat . EOS, because flew loses the probability that sat gains.

How do we differentiate expected reward when there are many responses?

Differentiating expected reward

For a fixed prompt, the objective is a sum over all possible completed responses. The scores are fixed, so only the probabilities are differentiated:

Jx(θ) = ∑o πθ(o | x) rϕ(x, o)
∇θ Jx(θ) = ∑o rϕ(x, o) ∇θ πθ(o | x)

The symbol ∇θ denotes the vector of partial derivatives with respect to the generator parameters. In the example it becomes d/dz. Using the reference values, sat . EOS contributes 2 × 0.1875 = 0.375 and flew . EOS contributes (−1) × (−0.1875) = 0.1875. Both terms are positive. Lowering the probability of a negative-reward response also raises expected reward. The sum, 0.5625, equals the slope.

With two responses, the exact sum has two terms. A language model is different. With V token choices at each of m positions there are Vm strings: about 1094 for V = 50,000 and m = 20. Each term would need a reward-model score and a gradient, so the exact sum cannot be enumerated.

Can we write this sum as an average over responses from the policy?

From a sum to a sample average

A probability-weighted sum is an expectation, and an expectation can be estimated from samples. Draw N responses from p and average f over them:

∑o p(o) f(o) = 𝔼o ∼ p[f(o)] ≈ 1N N∑i = 1 f(oi),oi ∼ p

ML01 revisited

The expected reward weights each reward by its policy probability. Four sampled responses, sat, sat, sat and flew, have rewards 2, 2, 2 and −1. Their average is 1.25, the expected reward, and no response list was needed.

Sampling needs two things. The weights must form a distribution we can sample from, and f must be computable for each sampled response. The work is then N generations, scores and gradients, however many responses are possible. The accuracy of the estimate depends on N and on the spread of f, not on the size of the response space.

The gradient sum fails the first requirement. Its weights ∇θ πθ(o | x) are not a distribution: some are negative, and they sum to zero because the probabilities always sum to 1. In the example they are +0.1875 and −0.1875. Multiplying and dividing each term by the response probability gives weights we can sample from:

∇θ Jx(θ) = ∑o πθ(o | x)[ rϕ(x, o) ∇θ πθ(o | x)πθ(o | x) ]

How do we compute the ratio ∇θ πθ / πθ for a sampled response?

The log-probability identity

For a response with positive probability, the chain rule for the natural logarithm gives exactly this ratio:

∇θ ln πθ(o | x) = ∇θ πθ(o | x)πθ(o | x)

Multiplying by the response probability gives the log-probability identity:

∇θ πθ(o | x) = πθ(o | x) ∇θ ln πθ(o | x)

The reference values confirm it: for sat . EOS, 0.75 × 0.25 = 0.1875, and for flew . EOS, 0.25 × (−0.75) = −0.1875. Substituting the identity into the gradient sum gives

∇θ Jx(θ) = ∑o πθ(o | x)[ rϕ(x, o) ∇θ ln πθ(o | x) ]

Each response term now has its policy probability as a weight. The logarithm appears because sampling needs the ratio of the probability gradient to the probability, and ∇θ ln πθ is that ratio.

What expectation does this probability-weighted sum represent?

The policy-gradient expectation

A sum weighted by the policy probabilities is an expectation under the policy. The gradient is the average of one quantity per response, g(o):

∇θ Jx(θ) = 𝔼o ∼ πθ(· | x)[ g(o) ],g(o) = rϕ(x, o) ∇θ ln πθ(o | x)

g(o) needs only the response itself: its reward and its log-probability gradient.

The two responses

sat . EOS, drawn with probability 0.75: g = 2 × 0.25 = 0.50. It raises the probability of a good response.

flew . EOS, drawn with probability 0.25: g = (−1) × (−0.75) = 0.75. It lowers the probability of a bad response, which also increases z.

Exact expectation: 0.75 × 0.50 + 0.25 × 0.75 = 0.5625. Three sampled responses, sat, sat and flew, give (0.50 + 0.50 + 0.75)/3 ≈ 0.58: close to the exact value, but not equal.

Across prompts, the same expectation also averages over the fixed prompt distribution from ML01:

∇θ J(θ) = 𝔼x ∼ Dprompt, o ∼ πθ(· | x)[ rϕ(x, o) ∇θ ln πθ(o | x) ]

How do we compute ∇θ ln πθ(o | x) for a sampled response?

Token log-probabilities

ML01 wrote a complete-response probability as a product of token probabilities. Taking the logarithm turns the product into a sum over every token, including EOS, and the gradient into a sum of token gradients:

ln πθ(o | x) = m∑t = 1 ln πθ(ot | x, o<t),om = EOS
∇θ ln πθ(o | x) = m∑t = 1 ∇θ ln πθ(ot | x, o<t)

For a fixed sampled sequence, one forward and backward pass computes every token term, as in next-token training. In the example only the first token depends on z: its derivative is 0.25, and the full stop and EOS contribute 0.

Comparison with SFT (Module 4)

SFT. For a human-written response o*, the update follows ∑t ∇θ ln πθ(o*t | x, o*<t). It maximises the sum of the target tokens’ log-probabilities.

Policy gradient. For a sampled response o ∼ πθ(· | x), the term is rϕ(x, o) times the same sum. Every token’s gradient term carries the same weight, the reward. A positive reward pushes the response’s total log-probability up; a negative reward pushes it down.

One policy-gradient step is therefore SFT on the model’s own sample, weighted by its reward. The same reward multiplies every token term, so it does not say which token mattered.

From the policy gradient to an algorithm

ML02 gave the exact gradient as an expectation under the policy. Turning it into an algorithm raises three problems, each with a standard solution.

How do we estimate the expectation without summing over every response?

Sampling: the REINFORCE estimate

The expectation still sums over every response. We replace it by a sample, as the sum-to-sample section of ML02 proposed.

Draw one response from the current policy and evaluate the quantity inside the expectation:

ĝ = rϕ(x, o) ∇θ ln πθ(o | x),o ∼ πθ(· | x)
𝔼o ∼ πθ(· | x)[ĝ] = ∇θ Jx(θ)

The second line says that the estimate is unbiased: averaged over the policy’s own samples it equals the exact gradient. This is the policy-gradient expectation from ML02 read in the other direction. Sutton and Barto call the resulting algorithm REINFORCE (§13.3, Eq. 13.8); it is a Monte Carlo method because it waits for a complete episode, here a complete response, before using its return.

Where the name REINFORCE comes from

Williams (1992) named the algorithm with a backronym: REward Increment = Nonnegative Factor × Offset Reinforcement × Characteristic Eligibility. Each factor has a counterpart in this microlecture:

Δθ = α (r − b) ∇θ ln πθ(o | x)

  • Nonnegative factor: the step size α.
  • Offset reinforcement: the reward minus a baseline, r − b.
  • Characteristic eligibility: the log-probability gradient ∇θ ln πθ(o | x).

The baseline was part of the original formulation. This section uses b = 0; the baselines section below supplies the offset.

The example

With rewards 2 and −1 at p = 0.75 the reference values from ML02 give two possible estimates. A sampled sat . EOS gives ĝ = 2 × 0.25 = 0.50. A sampled flew . EOS gives ĝ = (−1) × (−0.75) = 0.75. The first occurs with probability 0.75 and the second with probability 0.25, so the policy-weighted mean is 0.75 × 0.50 + 0.25 × 0.75 = 0.5625, the exact slope.

One sampled update proceeds as follows.

  1. Draw a response o ∼ πθ(· | x).
  2. Score it with the frozen reward model.
  3. Compute ĝ by backpropagation, using the gradient of r ∑t ln πθ(ot | x, o<t) . This is the reward-weighted SFT gradient from ML02.
  4. Update: θ ← θ + α ĝ.

Each step costs one generation, one reward-model call and one backward pass. Nothing in it depends on the number of possible responses, so sampling avoids enumerating the response space.

What does one update do to the example policy?

One update in the example

Take a step of size α = 0.1 along the exact gradient 0.5625. This is the average that a very large batch would give.

A single sample gives a different step. A sampled sat moves z by 0.1 × 0.50 and gives Jx = 1.2778. A sampled flew moves it by 0.1 × 0.75 and gives 1.2914. Both raise expected reward here, but by different amounts. Learning proceeds by many such noisy steps; on average they follow the exact gradient. This is stochastic gradient ascent, the same scheme used for every network trained in this course, with the noise now coming from which responses were sampled.

Repeated updates

Repeating the update keeps moving probability towards sat. The mean gradient estimate, 3p(1 − p), shrinks as p approaches 1, and Jx approaches its maximum of 2. Individual sampled estimates need not shrink. A sampled sat gives 2(1 − p), which does shrink. A sampled flew gives (−1) × (−p) = p, which approaches 1. It becomes rare, so the mean still shrinks.

How noisy is a single-sample estimate, and how does a batch help?

Exact expectations and sample estimates

Keep the policy fixed at p = 0.75. In the example, write ĝz for the estimate of dJx/dz. A sampled sat gives 0.50, and a sampled flew gives 0.75.

Exact and sampled

Exact expectation. With two responses, the expectation can be computed exactly: 0.75(0.50) + 0.25(0.75) = 0.5625.

One batch of four. The draws sat, flew, sat, flew give ḡz,4 = ¼(0.50 + 0.75 + 0.50 + 0.75) = 0.625. Four sat responses would instead give 0.50.

In general, with 𝒪x the set of all completed responses to x, the exact gradient and the batch estimate are

∇θ Jx =∑o ∈ 𝒪x πθ(o | x)rϕ(x, o) ∇θ ln πθ(o | x)
ḡN = 1NN∑i = 1 rϕ(x, oi)∇θ ln πθ(oi | x),oi ∼ πθ(· | x) independently

Individual batches can differ from the exact gradient. Across batches, 𝔼[ḡN] = ∇θ Jx. The batch update is θ ← θ + α ḡN.

How does batch size affect the variation in these estimates?

Batches: reducing the noise

An unbiased estimate can still be far from its mean on any one draw. In the example, ĝ is a scalar estimate of the slope dJx/dz, and its spread is measured by the variance:

Var(ĝ) = 𝔼o ∼ πθ(· | x)[(ĝ − dJx/dz)2]

In the example the two values are 0.50 and 0.75 around the mean 0.5625:

Var(ĝ) =0.75 × (−0.0625)2+ 0.25 × (0.1875)2= 0.0117

The standard deviation is about 0.11, small compared with the mean. Averaging N independent samples divides the variance by N and the standard deviation by √N. Halving the noise therefore needs four times as many samples.

Noise matters because it limits the step size. A large step along a noisy estimate can move the policy in a poor direction and lower expected reward. Noisy estimates therefore force small steps, large batches, or both, and either way learning slows down. For a language model, each extra sample costs a full generation and a reward-model call, so the batch cannot grow cheaply.

What happens to the noise if every reward is shifted by a constant?

Reward offsets can increase gradient variance

Add 10 to both rewards, so that sat . EOS scores 12 and flew . EOS scores 9. The reward difference is still 3, so the expected reward is shifted by 10 and its gradient does not change. The single-sample estimate changes a great deal:

Single-sample estimates with rewards 12 and 9
Sampled responseProbabilityrd ln π/dzĝ
sat . EOS0.75120.253.00

The mean is 0.75 × 3.00 + 0.25 × (−6.75) = 0.5625, as before. The variance rises from 0.0117 to 17.8, a standard deviation of 4.2, more than seven times the mean. Even a batch of 16 leaves a standard deviation of 1.06.

The reason is visible in the sign of each estimate. Because both rewards are positive, every sample pushes up its own probability. A sampled flew now gives a negative estimate and pushes z down, towards flew. The correct direction emerges only on average: the contributions are 0.75 × 3 = 2.25 from sat and 0.25 × (−6.75) = −1.6875 from flew, which leave the positive mean 0.5625. Most of each estimate is the shared offset, multiplied by a log-probability gradient whose average is zero.

Such offsets are not hypothetical. The Bradley–Terry loss in Module 5 depends only on reward differences, so it fixes a reward model’s scores only up to an added constant. Whatever constant training happened to produce passes straight into the variance of REINFORCE.

How can we remove the offset without changing the expected gradient?

Baselines

Subtract from the reward a value b(x) that may depend on the prompt but not on the sampled response:

ĝ = (rϕ(x, o) − b(x)) ∇θ ln πθ(o | x)

The subtracted term has expectation zero, so the estimate stays unbiased:

𝔼o ∼ πθ(· | x)[b(x) ∇θ ln πθ(o | x)]
= b(x) ∑o ∇θ πθ(o | x)= b(x) ∇θ 1 = 0

The step uses the log-probability identity from ML02 and the fact that the response probabilities sum to 1. It fails if b depends on the sampled response, because then b cannot be taken outside the sum.

The mean-reward baseline

With rewards 12 and 9, take b = 0.75 × 12 + 0.25 × 9 = 11.25, the expected reward under the current policy. A sampled sat gives (12 − 11.25) × 0.25 = 0.1875. A sampled flew gives (9 − 11.25) × (−0.75) = 1.6875. The mean is still 0.5625, and the variance falls from 17.8 to 0.4219.

Every sample now pushes in the correct direction. sat scored above the baseline, so its probability is raised. flew scored below it, so its probability is lowered. The difference r − b measures how much better than expected a response was.

The variance with this baseline does not depend on the offset. Adding any constant to both rewards also adds it to b, and the difference r − b is unchanged.

The mean-reward baseline does not always reduce variance. With the original rewards 2 and −1, the mean baseline b = 1.25 gives the same values 0.1875 and 1.6875, and the variance increases from 0.0117 to 0.4219. Without a baseline, those rewards happened to give low variance, because the two estimates, 0.50 and 0.75, are close together. The mean is not the variance-minimising baseline. It is used because it removes whatever offset the reward model has, and that is the large effect.

Optional: the variance-minimising baseline

The variance is a quadratic in b. Writing s(o) = d ln π/dz, its minimum is at b* = 𝔼[s2r] / 𝔼[s2]: a reward average weighted by s2, not the plain average. In the example b* = 9.75 for rewards 12 and 9, and −0.25 for rewards 2 and −1. With only two possible responses this baseline makes both estimates equal to 0.5625 and the variance zero. A single baseline generally cannot make every estimate identical. b* also depends on the parameters, so it is rarely computed exactly.

How do we estimate the expected reward of a prompt when its responses cannot be enumerated?

Estimating the baseline

In the example we computed the mean reward 11.25 from two known rewards and their probabilities. For a language model, the expected reward of a prompt is a sum over every response, which cannot be enumerated either. It does not need to be exact. Any b(x) that ignores the sampled response keeps ĝ unbiased, so an estimate of the expected reward is enough.

The standard choice, following Sutton and Barto (§13.4, v̂(St, w)), is a learned baseline bψ(x): a small model, often an extra output head on the language model, that takes the prompt and predicts its expected reward. It is fitted by regression on the rewards the policy actually receives:

Lb = 1NN∑i = 1 (bψ(xi) − ri)2

The minimiser of a squared error is the mean, so bψ(x) estimates 𝔼[r | x]. For a single prompt with sampled rewards 12, 12, 9 and 12, the best constant is their mean, 11.25, the baseline used above.

Why a learned baseline works

It sees only the prompt, never the sampled response, so the estimate stays unbiased.

Prompts rarely repeat, so the mean for one prompt cannot be measured by averaging many of its rewards. Each pair (xi, ri) is one noisy measurement of 𝔼[r | x], and the regression pools these measurements across similar prompts, as a surrogate model fitted to scattered data would.

The expected reward changes as the policy improves, so bψ is refitted on each batch. An inaccurate or lagging baseline costs some variance reduction; it never biases the update.

How do we combine sampling, averaging and a baseline in one update?

REINFORCE with a baseline

Sampling, batching and a learned baseline combine into one update per fresh batch. The reward model stays frozen; the baseline bψ(x) is a small learned predictor of the expected reward for each prompt.

  1. Sample a batch. Draw xi ∼ Dprompt and oi ∼ πθ(· | xi) for i = 1, …, N. Keep θ fixed while collecting the batch.
  2. Score and predict. ri = rϕ(xi, oi) and bi = bψ(xi). Use the baseline prediction before fitting it on this batch.
  3. Form advantages. Ai = ri − bi. Treat them as constants during the policy update.
  4. Update the policy. Minimise the loss below with θ ← θ − α ∇θLπ. At the current policy, −∇θLπ is an unbiased estimate of the expected-reward gradient, so the update performs stochastic gradient ascent. A single sampled update need not improve expected reward.
  5. Fit the baseline. Minimise the squared error below with ψ ← ψ − β ∇ψLb.
  6. Repeat with a fresh batch from the updated policy.
Lπ = −1NN∑i = 1 AiTi∑t = 1 ln πθ(oi,t | xi, oi,<t)
Lb = 1NN∑i = 1 (bψ(xi) − ri)2

Ti counts the response tokens of oi, including EOS. Prompt and padding tokens are excluded from the sum.

Supplementary: credit assignment and actor–critic methods

Optional reading. Not required for Module 7.

REINFORCE with a baseline multiplies every token’s log-probability gradient by the same number, r − b. It cannot tell which tokens made the response good. Sutton and Barto’s actor–critic methods (§13.5) address this. A learned value function, the critic, estimates the expected return from each intermediate state, here each prefix. The policy, the actor, is then updated token by token with the one-step signal δt = rt + V̂(st+1) − V̂(st), with V̂ = 0 after EOS. Rewards before EOS are zero, so for intermediate tokens δt is the change in the critic’s estimate. At EOS, δt = r − V̂(st), where the response reward arrives.

Using estimates of later values in this way is called bootstrapping. Bootstrapping can reduce variance, and it can introduce bias, because the critic’s estimates are imperfect. Generalised advantage estimation interpolates between the two extremes. These methods appear inside PPO, which Module 7 discusses at the level of concepts.
